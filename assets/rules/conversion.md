# Conversion

How a page is arranged so a visitor who arrives with a doubt leaves having acted. This is the site's purpose; `design` and `content` serve it. Applies to every site whose brief names an action, including a portfolio (the action is "get in touch") and a nonprofit (donate, volunteer, sign).

## Principles

- **One page, one action.** Every page has exactly one primary action. Secondary actions exist, visibly subordinate; they never compete.
- **The page is an argument.** Its sections are a sequence of claims, each with its proof, in the order a visitor's doubts arise. The plan writes this order; the copy and the design follow it. A section that makes no claim is cut.
- **Answer the doubt where it occurs.** Trust evidence — reviews, credentials, numbers, guarantees, names — sits beside the claim it supports, not in a "Testimonials" section at the bottom.
- **Nothing stands between the visitor and the content.** No preloader, no interstitial, no popup before they have read anything, no cookie wall where the law doesn't require one.
- **Speed is conversion.** Every second of load loses visitors; `performance`'s budgets exist for this reason.

## The first screen

Within the first screen, on a phone, without scrolling: what the business does, who it is for, the one thing that makes it the better choice, and the primary action. A visitor who leaves after three seconds should still know those four things.

## The action

- The primary action appears in the first screen and again at natural decision points — after the proof, at the end — not pasted between every section.
- Its label is the same everywhere, and says what happens (`content` → Calls to action).
- Contact is always within reach: under two clicks from anywhere on a multi-page site, never more than one section away on a landing page. A phone number, where the business takes calls, is a tappable link in the header or footer.
- Where the action is a call or a message rather than a form, the page says when someone answers.

## Landing pages

A landing page has no exploratory navigation — no header menu, no footer link columns. Its whole structure is one argument toward one action. Legal links and contact stay in the footer.

## Forms

- The fewest fields that let the business follow up. Every extra field costs submissions. Name, one way to reply, and the question is usually enough; qualifying details are asked in the reply.
- What happens after submit is specified and built: the confirmation state, what the visitor should expect, and when. A submission that fails to deliver shows the visitor another way to get in touch and alerts the owner.
- A form the visitor can't finish on a phone in a minute is too long.

## Pricing

Where the business publishes prices, they are on the site, plainly, with what's included. Where it can't, the page says how pricing works and what a visitor gets by asking. "Contact us for pricing" with no explanation is a doubt left unanswered.

## Multi-page sites

- Every page is an entrance. A visitor from a search result or an ad lands anywhere, so each page states what the business is and carries the action, not only the home page.
- The home page's job is to route: it makes the main claim and sends each kind of visitor to the page that answers them.
- Service and product pages answer one intent each; the plan's SEO map decides which.

## Measuring

The primary action on each page fires an analytics event (`analytics`). That number is how the owner knows the site works; a site without it can't be improved.
