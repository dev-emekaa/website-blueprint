# Content

How the site's words are written: the copy that makes a visitor trust the business, and the microcopy that guides them without friction. The page's argument is in `conversion`; the voice is in the brief.

## Principles

- **Words are the product.** A visitor reads before they look. Copy is written first, in a deck, and the design wraps it; it is never filled in after the layout.
- **From the visitor's side.** Write in the words a customer would use, about what they get, not about the business. "Your roof fixed in a day" beats "We are a leading roofing provider."
- **Plain beats clever.** Describe what something is or does. Clever phrasing that doesn't quite make sense reads as machine-made. Expressive visuals do not need poetic copy.
- **Say less.** Every element does one job. Text goes only where it earns its place; an image, a number or structure carries the meaning where it can.
- **True, or not there.** No invented customers, quotes, logos, counts, awards or credentials. Where the owner hasn't supplied proof, the page makes the claim without the proof, and the gap is listed for them.

## Voice

The brief records the voice in three or four plain adjectives and what it must not sound like. A law firm and a tattoo studio do not sound alike; neither sounds like a startup. Every deck is checked against those words before it is presented.

## Headlines

- The headline says what this is, for whom, or what to do. Shapes that work: the claim ("Roofs that outlast the mortgage"), what it is ("Family dentistry in Leith"), the instruction ("Book a free survey"), the name with a line under it that explains.
- It must survive a phone. At hero size on a 390px screen, about 32 characters (four to six words) fits in two lines. Longer headlines wrap to three and lose. The supporting line is at most 22 words and never restates the headline.
- Shapes that fail: the mad-lib ("AI-powered platform for modern teams"), the tricolon ("Fast. Simple. Secure."), the correction ("Not just X — it's Y"), "Welcome to", "Meet", "reimagined", a question nobody asked.

## Body and sections

- Every section opens with a heading that makes its claim; the body proves it. A section with no claim has no reason to exist.
- Short paragraphs, one idea each. Lists for parallel items, prose for argument.
- Numbers are specific: "Open until 8pm", "14-day returns", "Since 1987". Vague superlatives ("best", "leading", "world-class") are deleted.
- Reading level: a 13-year-old follows it, whatever the industry. Jargon only where the audience uses it themselves.
- Words that mark generated copy are not used: unlock, seamless, elevate, effortless, cutting-edge, all-in-one, empower, leverage, delve, robust, journey (unless it is one), "in today's fast-paced world".

## Calls to action

- A button says exactly what happens: "Book a site visit", "Get a quote", "Call 0131 …". Never "Submit", "Learn more", "Get started", "Click here".
- One to three words, verb first. One label per action across the whole site: the same action always has the same name.
- The name carries through the flow: "Request a quote" → "Quote requested. We'll reply within one working day."
- Links name their destination: "See the pricing", not "here".

## Proof

- Testimonials carry a full name and something that makes them real — a company, a town, a photo the person supplied. Anonymous praise is weaker than none.
- Numbers come with their basis: "4.9 from 212 Google reviews", not "5-star rated".
- Credentials, licences and memberships are named exactly as the issuing body names them.

## Microcopy

- **Forms:** labels are visible, not placeholders. A description under a field says what format or why it's needed, only when that isn't obvious. Optional fields are marked optional; required ones aren't marked.
- **Errors** say what went wrong and how to fix it, beside the field, in a sentence: "Enter a phone number we can call back on." They don't apologise, don't say "oops", "please" or "invalid", and never blame.
- **Confirmation** says what happened and what comes next, with a time: "Message sent. We reply within one working day, Monday to Friday."
- **Empty and failure states** are invitations to act, with a way forward — a phone number when a form fails to send.
- **404:** says the page isn't here, offers the three most useful links and the search or contact. No joke the visitor didn't ask for.
- **Navigation labels** are nouns, five at most plus the action, each one the visitor's word for it.
- **Alt text** describes what the image shows and why it is there, in one sentence; decorative images get an empty alt.

## Mechanics

- Sentence case everywhere, including buttons and navigation. Typographic punctuation: curly quotes, en dashes for ranges, a non-breaking space in "10 km" and before a unit.
- Em dashes: one or two per page at most. Exclamation marks: none in errors, few anywhere.
- Store text in its natural case; the design sets any capitals.
- Dates, numbers and currency are formatted for the brief's market with `Intl`, never by hand.

## Meta copy

- Title: the page's subject first, the business name last, under about 60 characters so it isn't cut off. Home: what the business is and where.
- Description: one or two sentences that make the search result worth clicking, with the visitor's words, under about 155 characters. It is marketing copy, not a summary.
- Open Graph title and description may be warmer than the search ones; the image shows the business, not the logo on a gradient.
